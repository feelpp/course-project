Session 2 — 3D thermal fins on Gaya

M2 · 14 September 2026 · 2 hours · Mini-project in pairs.

Build and verify a heat-transfer simulation using an existing Python generator. You will produce the inputs, run the Feel++ Heat toolbox in Apptainer on Gaya, and compare runs with one and four MPI ranks before visualizing the results in ParaView.

This session builds on the Apptainer practical and prepares you for the simulation → data → training → inference → verification pipeline. You are responsible for creating the case files and Slurm script; the guidance below provides starting points and success criteria.

1. Objective of the mini-project

The goal is to develop the complete solution as a reproducible workflow, in a pair. The workflow starts from a parameter space and a sampling budget, generates one 3D case and mesh for each sampled point, launches the simulations, collects the quantity of interest, trains the team’s selected model, and verifies its predictions against finite-element results.

The workflow may run locally, on Gaya, or across both environments. What matters is that every transition is automated and documented: inputs → sampling → case and mesh generation → Slurm/Apptainer execution → collection on Gaya → dataset → training → inference → verification. A manual operation is acceptable for an exploratory check, but it must not be required for the reproducible campaign.

2. Physical interpretation

The case represents a CPU heating the lower face of a central post. The heat is conducted through the post and the four horizontal fins, then transferred to the ambient air from the exposed surfaces. Only the solid Heat problem is solved; the air flow is not meshed or solved.

The generator uses nondimensional variables. The CPU boundary carries the prescribed heat flux, while the ambient exchange is represented by a convective (Robin) condition whose nondimensional coefficient is the Biot number Bi. Use the generator’s definitions and signs when interpreting the results, and document the reference scales before giving any dimensional interpretation.

3. Geometry, mesh and temperature field

The following two views illustrate the expected outputs of the numerical stage. They are complementary: the mesh shows the discrete finite-element problem, while the temperature field shows the computed physical response.

Figure 1. Figure 1. 3D mesh. The volume is discretized with tetrahedral elements around the central post and the four fins. Before launching a campaign, check the element quality, the four-fin geometry, the Gamma_root marker and all external markers. A successful meshing command alone is not evidence that the physical groups required by the Heat model are correct.
Figure 2. 3D temperature visualization. The warmest region is expected near the heated CPU/root and the post; temperature decreases along the fins as heat is transferred to the ambient. The colour scale and the nondimensional convention must be recorded. This view is for physical interpretation and debugging; the scalar used by the MLOps model must come from the documented average on Gamma_root.

For each sampled parameter vector, the workflow should preserve both the generated mesh and the visualization-ready result. The mesh is an input to the solver; the temperature field is an output used for inspection and, together with the root statistic, for verification.

4. From simulations to a learned mapping

Denote a parameter point by \(\mu=(L,t,\mathrm{Bi})\). The solver associates with it the functional

\[S(\mu)=\overline{T}_{\mathrm{root}}(\mu),\]

the mean temperature on the heated CPU/post root. Learning does not replace the finite-element solver during data generation: it learns the mapping \(\mu \mapsto S(\mu)\) from the verified simulation database. This scalar \(S\) is the typical quantity of interest used for training, prediction and error assessment; the complete 3D temperature field is retained for visualization and scientific checks.

As a final application, the validated surrogate can support rapid optimization. For a design study, search over \((L,t,\mathrm{Bi})\) for the admissible configuration with the lowest base temperature (or another explicitly stated objective). For an operational study, fix the geometry and optimize the permitted operating parameter(s) to meet a target or limit. The selected candidate must always be confirmed by a new finite-element run.

5. Organization and objectives

Alternate roles: one person operates the tools while the other reviews the inputs and checks the results. Each partner must be able to explain and repeat the pair’s work.

Running with one rank, then four ranks, and comparing the results is the core assignment. The subsequent MLOps campaign uses the same 3D configuration with four fins and varies L, t and Bi.

6. Prepare your workspace

Reuse your SSH/VS Code connection and the Gaya guidance. The instructor supplies the site-specific details: Slurm partition/account, modules, shared storage and the SIF image path. They also specify the MPI launch method compatible with this image and whether the native Feel++ installation is available.

Your tasks:

  1. Organize separate directories for the generator, cases and job results.

  2. Check the Apptainer version and identify the image being used, including its checksum.

  3. Prepare an isolated Python environment for the generator.

  4. Check how to request a Slurm allocation. Meshing and solving must run on an allocated compute node, not the login node.

Use the prepared SIF rather than spending the session downloading or building an image. The generator requires python-liquid (version 2.1.0 was used to verify this material), imported as liquid. It does not require the Feel++ Python bindings.

7. Explore the thermal-fin generator

The official script is in the feelpp/feelpp repository:

toolboxes/generator/cases/thermal_fin/case_generator_thermal_fin.py.

Clone the feelpp/feelpp repository into your workspace and locate the generator directory. Use revision 44487393868854b53e47e82217d3a0fbc557c8d1 to share a common reference with the deployment guide, and record the checked-out commit. Preserve the script and templates/ layout. No compilation of Feel++ is required for this step: the clone provides the generator sources, while the installed toolbox or SIF provides the solver.

First check whether case_generator_thermal_fin.py is already available locally, and distinguish an installed tool from a file in a source checkout. Either way, use your own clone for this practical so you can inspect the templates and track your changes without modifying a shared installation.

Start by exploring the interface:

python case_generator_thermal_fin.py --help

7.1. Read before running

Answer briefly in your report:

  1. How does argparse obtain the parameters? What are their types and default values?

  2. How does the code select a 2D or 3D geometry? What does --cylinder change?

  3. What do FileSystemLoader and render() do? Why must you keep all the templates, including the one whose name contains crb?

  4. How does the number of fins affect both the geometry and the materials in the JSON?

The relevant options are --dim, --N, --L, --d, --t, --cylinder and --odir. Determine their meaning from the code and the manual. Where they differ, rely on the expressions actually generated, particularly for spacing and the dimensions of cylindrical shapes.

7.2. Generate the reference case

Construct the command yourself for the following case:

Record the command you ran. Examine the generated files and explain the difference between a .geo geometry, a .msh mesh, a .json model and a .cfg configuration. Does the generator already provide a mesh? What step is still needed?

Checkpoint: locate the groups Post, Fin_i, Gamma_root and Gamma_ext in the templates and then in the generated files, and explain how the model uses them.

8. Prepare a consistent Heat problem

We solve steady-state conduction in the solid. A boundary condition describing heat exchange with the surroundings does not imply that we solve the airflow: we use only the Heat toolbox.

Starting from the generated model:

  1. Identify the conductivities, the Bi parameter and the external temperature; distinguish the values used by Heat from the bounds intended for a parameter study.

  2. Identify the conditions at the base and on the external surfaces. Is a temperature or a flux prescribed at the base?

  3. Explain the meaning of the case’s normalized values. Do not report degrees Celsius or millimetres without defining a consistent physical scaling.

  4. Prepare a configuration for Heat. Separate solver options from those related to model order reduction; preserve the upstream files so you can explain your changes.

  5. Set up the mean root-temperature measure described in Measure and compare so it is available in both runs.

  6. Choose a mesh size suitable for a quick trial and generate a 3D mesh with Gmsh. The value h=0.2 is a starting point for this small case; it does not replace a convergence study.

Hints: read gmsh -help for the dimension, format and output-file options; use MSH 2 format for this practical. Gmsh must support OpenCASCADE, either in the image or in the provided native installation. Examine the messages and mesh quality, not just the exit code.

In Gmsh, check the volumes, physical groups and actual location of the base surface. A name appearing in the JSON does not prove that the correct set of elements is selected.

Next, configure the mesh import in the Heat JSON. Use the same mesh file to compare one and four ranks. The Meshes.heat.Import section and its partitioning option are described in the JSON model documentation. The $cfgdir symbol lets you reference a file relative to the CFG directory.

Checkpoint: another pair must be able to follow the CFG → JSON → mesh chain and verify that the required markers exist. Note that the upstream JSON contains // comments, which strict JSON validators do not accept.

9. Run Heat on Gaya

9.1. Build a first job using one rank

Write your Slurm script using what you learned in the previous session. It must:

  • Request modest resources for a one-rank run and preserve standard output and error logs.

  • Launch feelpp_toolbox_heat inside the image using apptainer exec.

  • Pass the CFG with --config-file and explicitly select the results directory with --directory.

  • Make the inputs accessible inside the container and keep the outputs on Gaya after execution.

  • Isolate each job’s results and record the environment and inputs used.

Consult the Heat toolbox interface and the first-session material if you get stuck. Consider paths as seen from the host and from the container. If importing the mesh creates partitioning files, provide a writable working copy of the inputs for each job.

Submit with sbatch, monitor with squeue, then examine the logs and, where available, sacct. Locate the result files without assuming they were written to the current directory.

Checkpoint: demonstrate solver convergence and a usable temperature field. A Slurm COMPLETED state alone does not validate the solution.

9.2. Adapt your launch to four ranks

Keep exactly the same problem, mesh and numerical settings. Adapt the Slurm request and launch to use four ranks on a single node.

Use the launcher validated by the instructor: the hybrid model starts processes with Gaya’s MPI, then runs Feel++ inside Apptainer. The host and image MPI libraries must be compatible; see Apptainer and MPI. For a direct srun launch, use the PMI/PMIx plugin validated for this combination.

Explain: why is increasing --ntasks alone insufficient? Why are four independent processes different from a four-rank MPI computation? What would happen if --cleanenv removed the MPI environment?

Do not use --mpi=none for the multirank computation. Check the communicator size in the Feel++ output; the exported pid field also helps inspect the partitions. Do not confuse this check with the number of tasks requested from Slurm.

9.3. Native alternative

If the instructor provides access to the native Feel++ installation on Gaya, adapt your script to invoke the Heat toolbox directly with the specified Feel++/MPI modules. Keep the same CFG, JSON and mesh, and continue to isolate job results.

Record the version and modules: the native installation and SIF are separate environments. This alternative also allows you to continue the parallel work if the image’s MPI has not yet been validated; state clearly which execution method you actually used.

10. Measure and compare

Add the mean_root_temperature surface-average measure on Gamma_root to the generated model using PostProcess.heat.Measures.Statistics, as shown in the project specification and the Feel++ JSON documentation. Locate its column in the generated CSV before launching the campaign.

The quantity of interest is the mean temperature on the root surface, \(\overline{T}_{\mathrm{root}}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma_{\mathrm{root}}|}\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{root}}} T\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma\). State the nondimensional convention. Why is the arithmetic mean of the nodal values not necessarily equivalent to this surface average?

Compare one and four ranks in a small table of your own design: job IDs, environment, mesh hash, rank count, convergence, mean temperature and timings. Define a relative difference and propose a justified tolerance to discuss with the instructor. If the results differ, examine the inputs, convergence and MPI launch before attributing the difference to a physical effect.

Distinguish queue time, startup, partitioning, solving and writing. A very small case may be slower with four ranks; explain this result if it occurs.

11. Explore with ParaView

Open the results in ParaView, either from the Gaya graphical session provided for the course or on your computer after transferring the output directory. The choice of a native or containerized solver does not dictate where the visualizer runs.

Hints: locate the exported entry file (.case for EnSight, or .pvd if VTK is configured). It references other files: transfer the entire required directory, not just this file or a single partition. See loading data in ParaView.

Create a visualization that lets you:

  1. View the mesh and temperature with a readable legend.

  2. Examine the interior using a slice through the post and fins.

  3. Identify the heated base and interpret the observed gradients.

  4. Visualize the partitions using the parallel run’s pid field.

  5. Compare one and four ranks using the same slice and colour scale.

Save an annotated screenshot and a ParaView state. Explain which files are still needed to reopen this state elsewhere. As an extension, investigate how to verify a volume average with the Integrate Variables filter.

12. Prepare the MLOps parameter study

Once the reference case has been verified, prepare the full campaign over exactly three parameters: fin length L, fin thickness t and Bi. Keep the number of fins equal to four and use the largest heat flux prescribed for the campaign. Other generator/model parameters, including the fixed post setting, must be recorded and held constant.

Use a documented seeded sampling strategy over the approved three-dimensional domain. Predict the qualitative effect of each parameter and decide when a new 3D mesh is required. Create each case in a separate directory, preserve parameters and mesh checksums, then submit the campaign through a Slurm job array. Start with a smoke subset before the full design.

Propose a data-row schema linking (L, t, Bi) to mean_root_temperature and its provenance. Split the completed samples into disjoint train, validation and test sets before training. One-rank and four-rank runs of the same case are numerical verification runs, not two independent physical examples to split between training and testing.

13. End-of-session deliverables

  • Your commands, scripts and configurations, with the generator revision and an explanation of your changes.

  • Job IDs and logs, measurements and their comparison; keep the mesh and results in the designated storage space with their checksums.

  • A ParaView screenshot with a legend and physical interpretation.

  • A short report stating what works, the obstacles encountered and the remaining checks. Report a failed MPI run as a failure.

Another pair must be able to reproduce your workflow from these materials. Version scripts and small configuration files in Git; keep the SIF and large outputs in the designated storage space.
